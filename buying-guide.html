Skylight Buying Guide for Toronto Homeowners

The pre-purchase decisions that matter. Get these right and the install is straightforward; get them wrong and you regret it for 20 years.

Picking the right skylight isn't about brand. It's about matching the unit to the room, the roof, and the goal. Most "I regret my skylight" stories trace back to one of five decisions made wrong at the buying stage — not the installation stage. This guide covers each of those five.

Decision 1: Where is it going?

The room dictates the unit type. Mismatches:

  • Bathroom or kitchen — needs vented. Fixed unit in a humidity-heavy room creates chronic condensation. See residential skylight options
  • Stairwell or hallway — fixed is fine and cheapest. Vented is overkill
  • Small closet or pantry — sun tunnel beats a traditional skylight. Same daylight at lower cost. See sun tunnel skylights
  • Living room with vaulted ceiling — multiple fixed units in a row, possibly motorized blinds
  • Master bedroom — vented for fresh air; consider electric for hard-to-reach openings

Decision 2: What kind of roof do you have?

The roof type determines the install method and significantly affects cost:

  • Sloped asphalt — simplest. Deck-mounted skylight is standard
  • Flat roof — completely different system. Curb-mounted with proper membrane termination. See flat-roof skylights
  • Metal roof — requires custom flashing kit. Adds $200–500
  • Slate or tile — premium install. Costs reflect the complexity
  • Cedar shake — possible but specialty. Get a contractor who's done it

Decision 3: Fixed, manual vented, or electric vented?

Don't undersize on accessibility. A manual vented unit at 12 feet is closer to "unusable feature" than "skylight" — most homeowners stop bothering after the first month.

Decision 4: Glass package

This is where most homeowners overspec or underspec without knowing it:

  • Standard double-glazed — base unit. Works fine for most installations
  • Low-E coating — adds $100–200. Worth it. Cuts solar heat in summer, retains heat in winter
  • Laminated glass — adds $150–300. Worth it for safety. Code-required in many Toronto applications
  • Triple-glazed — adds $400–800. Only worth it in extreme climates or for sound reduction near busy roads
  • Solar-control glass — adds $200–400. Worth it on south- or west-facing openings to reduce summer heat gain

Decision 5: Smart features and accessories

The accessory category is where the brand difference matters most:

  • Manual blinds and shades — adds $200–400 per unit. Most homeowners regret skipping these
  • Motorized blinds — adds $400–800. Often worth it on high or hard-to-reach units
  • Rain sensor (auto-close) — comes with most solar/electric vented units. Genuinely useful
  • Smart-home integration — Velux Active is the most mature ecosystem. See smart-home skylight systems. See also the accessories guide

Common mistakes

  • Cheaping out on the unit, spending big on labour — backwards. The unit lasts 20+ years; the labour is one-time
  • Picking based on the lowest quote — usually missing flashing kit or proper ice-and-water shield
  • Overshooting size — bigger skylights look great in photos, but in real rooms they often create heat issues
  • Picking a fixed unit because it's cheaper, in a humid room — pay for vented or be ready to repaint the ceiling shaft annually
  • Forgetting the interior shaft work — drywall, paint, sometimes electrical. Add $300–700 to the total
  • Skipping motorized blinds because of cost — easier to add at install than retrofit later

Questions to ask every installer

  • What brand and model do you recommend, and why?
  • Are you using the manufacturer flashing kit, or fabricating your own?
  • Will you install ice-and-water shield around the curb?
  • Will my opening need a permit? Who pulls it?
  • Does the quote include interior drywall and finish work?
  • What's the warranty on the unit and on your installation?
  • Can I see 2-3 recent similar installs?
